Inventor of the World Wide Web

Tim Berners-Lee

Architect of the Open Web

He turned a networked collection of documents into a shared space for humanity, then gave it away so that anyone, anywhere could build on it.

Read His Story

From Curious Engineer to Web Pioneer

Timothy John Berners-Lee was born in London on 8 June 1955. Both of his parents worked as programmers on the Ferranti Mark 1, one of the first commercial computers, so technology was part of daily conversation at home. He went on to study physics at The Queen's College, Oxford, graduating with first-class honours in 1976, and famously built his first computer from a soldering iron, a processor and an old television set.14

In 1980 he took a short contract at CERN, the European physics laboratory near Geneva. Researchers there came from all over the world, and their information lived on different computers in incompatible formats. To keep track of people, projects and software, he wrote a personal program called ENQUIRE, which linked pieces of information together, and the seed of a bigger idea took root.12

In March 1989 he wrote a proposal for a universal, linked information system, and his manager noted on the cover that it was "vague but exciting". Working with Robert Cailliau, he refined the idea, and by the end of 1990 he had written the first web browser and web server on a NeXT computer. He had also defined the three foundations that still underpin the web today: HTML, URLs and HTTP.23

His most consequential decision came next. On 30 April 1993, CERN placed the web's underlying software in the public domain, free for anyone to use without royalties. In 1994 he founded the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) at MIT to develop open standards, so that the web would work for everyone on every kind of device. That commitment to openness is why the web became a global platform rather than a proprietary product.31

Key Moments

  1. 1980

    Builds ENQUIRE at CERN

    A personal hypertext program for linking people, projects and software becomes the starting point for his later ideas.

  2. 1989

    Proposes the web

    He submits his proposal for a linked information management system, the blueprint of the World Wide Web.

  3. 1990

    First browser and server

    He completes the first web browser and server, defining HTML, URLs and HTTP along the way.

  4. 1993

    Released royalty-free

    On 30 April, CERN places the web software in the public domain, opening it to everyone.

  5. 1994

    Founds the W3C

    The World Wide Web Consortium is established at MIT to guide the web through open standards.

  6. 2001

    Elected Fellow of the Royal Society

    The UK's national academy of sciences recognises his contribution to computing and the web.

  7. 2004

    Knighted and honoured

    He receives a knighthood and the inaugural Millennium Technology Prize for inventing the web.

  8. 2007

    Awarded the Order of Merit

    He receives the Order of Merit, a personal honour awarded by the British monarch.

  9. 2009

    World Wide Web Foundation launches

    The foundation is established to coordinate efforts to extend the web's benefits to humanity.

  10. 2013

    Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering

    On 18 March he shares the inaugural prize with Vint Cerf, Robert Kahn, Louis Pouzin and Marc Andreessen.

  11. 2017

    ACM A.M. Turing Award

    ACM announces him as the 2016 recipient of computing's highest honour, recognising the invention of the web, the first browser and the protocols that let it scale.

  12. 2019

    Contract for the Web

    In November, a global plan of action for a safer, more open and more equitable web is launched, following his call for one on the web's 30th anniversary.

  13. 2022

    Seoul Peace Prize

    He is honoured for his work on data sovereignty and for leading efforts to decentralise a web dominated by large technology companies.

  14. 2025

    Publishes This Is for Everyone

    In September he releases his book, adding to his long-running writing on the web's design and future.

Dates are drawn from sources 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 below.

Why It Still Matters

An Open Platform

Releasing the web royalty-free meant anyone could publish, build and innovate without asking permission. Every website and web application relies on that decision.

Universal Access

Through the W3C, open standards and accessibility guidelines help ensure the web works across devices, languages and abilities.

A Web That Serves People

He has campaigned for privacy, fairness and user control through the Contract for the Web and the Solid project, which aims to give people ownership of their own data.68
